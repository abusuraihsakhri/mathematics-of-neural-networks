Chapter 1

Foundations and notation

Before any derivative: what kind of object a neural network is, what training actually asks of it, and the index conventions that make the later algebra come out clean.

In Simple Terms · The Big Picture

What are we actually doing here?

  • A neural network is a mathematical assembly line: You give it numbers (an image, sounds, sensor readings), it runs them through successive layers that multiply, add, and filter, and outputs a final prediction.
  • Training is adjusting dials: The weights (\(w\)) and biases (\(b\)) are millions of adjustable knobs. Training is the process of wiggling those knobs until the output prediction matches reality.
  • The Core Challenge: If you turn a knob on layer 2 by a millimeter, how much does the mistake at layer 50 change? Answering that question for all million knobs at once, while steering high-dimensional parameters down complex loss valleys, is what the mathematics in this guide accomplishes across its three core pillars: Linear Algebra, Matrix Calculus, and Optimization Theory.

The Three Pillars of Neural Network Mathematics

This guide develops the complete mathematical machinery underlying modern neural networks. Rather than treating deep learning as black-box heuristics or isolated calculus formulas, we unify the three mathematical pillars that make learning possible:

1. Linear algebra & high-dimensional geometry. Vectors, matrices, transposes, inner products, and matrix affine mappings \(z = W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}\). Networks manipulate data by rotating, shearing, stretching, and contracting coordinate manifolds. If you can multiply a \(3\times2\) matrix by a \(2\times1\) vector and understand why the result is \(3\times1\), you have enough to begin.

2. Multivariable calculus & matrix analysis. Gradients, Jacobians, and the chain rule on directed computational graphs. Gradients give directional slopes; Jacobians measure how multi-dimensional transformations stretch space; and adjoint sensitivity vectors pull backward through transposed Jacobians (\(J^\top\)). Chapter 2 rebuilds this entire toolkit visually from scratch.

3. Optimization theory & loss landscapes. Gradient descent dynamics, learning rate stability bounds (\(\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}\)), condition numbers, heavy-ball momentum, and stochastic mini-batch noise escaping saddle points. Optimization bridges pure calculus with physical convergence.

Worth knowing

The mathematics here is old. The perceptron dates to 1958, the calculus of training multi-layer networks was circulating through the 1960s and 70s, and the paper that made backpropagation famous is Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams (1986). What changed after 2012 was data, hardware and engineering — not these equations. The derivations on the next six pages are the same ones running inside a modern framework, just with more layers and a smarter optimiser wrapped around them.

A network is a function

Strip away the diagrams of circles and arrows and a feed-forward neural network is one large function assembled from many small ones. It takes a vector in and returns something out — a number, a vector of class probabilities, a decision.

\[ \hat y = f(\mathbf x;\theta) = f_L \circ f_{L-1} \circ \cdots \circ f_2 \circ f_1(\mathbf x) \]

Physical Intuition: The Audio Mixing Console & Filter Bank

Think of a multi-track audio mixing console in a recording studio:

  • The raw instruments (guitar, vocals, drums) are the input signals \(\mathbf x\).
  • The sliding faders on each channel determine how loudly each instrument contributes to a combined sub-mix — these are the weights \(W\).
  • The preamp gain knobs add a baseline DC offset or master boost — these are the biases \(b\).
  • A hard-clipping audio limiter or distortion pedal clips and shapes the sound non-linearly so it doesn't blow out the speakers — that is the activation function \(\phi\).
  • The sound engineer listens to the master output and measures how muddy or noisy it is — that single measure of badness is the cost \(C\).

Training the network is simply fine-tuning all the slider faders until the master song sounds completely clean.

OUTPUT VU

Two different kinds of quantity appear in that expression and keeping them apart is the whole game:

  • The input \(\mathbf x\) is a vector holding all the variables of a single training example. It is given to us and we never change it.
  • The parameters \(\theta\) are all the weights and biases, however many there are. They are ours to choose, and choosing them well is training.

Each \(f_l\) is one layer: an affine map followed by a non-linear activation function. Nothing more exotic than that. The apparent complexity of a deep network comes from repeating a simple step many times, not from any single step being complicated.

Training asks exactly one question

Attach a cost function \(C\) to the output — some scalar measure of how wrong the prediction is against the known answer. Now the entire enterprise collapses into a minimisation problem in many variables:

\[ \min_{\theta}\; C(\theta) \]

And the way we attack a differentiable minimisation problem is with derivatives. So the practical question, asked once per parameter, is:

The question

For every single weight \(w\) and every bias \(b\) in the network: how much does the cost change when I nudge this parameter? That is \(\partial C/\partial w\) and \(\partial C/\partial b\) — and computing all of them efficiently is precisely what backpropagation does.

Suppose a small network has a thousand parameters. We want a thousand partial derivatives. Together they tell us how much each parameter contributes to the current error, which tells us which ones to increase and which to decrease. Everything from chapter 2 onward exists to compute that thousand-entry vector without doing a thousand separate pieces of work.

Frame to keep

Backpropagation is not a special algorithm invented for neural networks. It is the chain rule, organised so that a composed function with millions of parameters can be differentiated in roughly the cost of evaluating it once. The organising is the contribution. The calculus is ordinary.

The superscript on a weight is where it is going

This is the convention that trips people. \(W^l\) does not mean "the weights of layer \(l\)" in a vague sense. It means the weights arriving at layer \(l\), which is to say the weights that leave layer \(l-1\). Weights live on the edges between layers, and we name each edge after its destination.

A single weight carries two more indices:

\[ w^l_{jk}\;:\;\text{the weight from neuron } k \text{ in layer } l-1 \text{ to neuron } j \text{ in layer } l \]

Destination first, source second. That reads backwards, and it is worth knowing why it is written that way.

Why destination first

Collect the \(w^l_{jk}\) into a matrix with \(j\) indexing rows and \(k\) indexing columns. Then the weighted sum arriving at neuron \(j\) is \(\sum_k w^l_{jk}a^{l-1}_k\) — which is exactly row \(j\) of \(W^l\) dotted with the vector \(a^{l-1}\). Stack all the rows and the whole layer is the single product \(W^l a^{l-1}\), with no transpose anywhere.

Index the other way round and every layer equation picks up a \(W\T\). The awkward-looking convention is chosen to make the important formula clean.

Figure 1.2: The weight index convention: \(w^l_{jk}\) arrives at neuron \(j\) in layer \(l\), having departed neuron \(k\) in layer \(l-1\). Row \(j\) in matrix \(W^l\) collects all inputs arriving at neuron \(j\).
Shape check

\(W^l\) is \(n_l \times n_{l-1}\): rows = neurons it feeds, columns = neurons it comes from. So a layer taking 5 inputs down to 3 neurons has a \(3\times5\) weight matrix. If you ever lose your place in a derivation, recover it by writing the shapes.

Biases

Each neuron adds a bias to its weighted sum before the activation function. Written per neuron that is \(b^l_j\); stacked over the layer it is the vector \(b^l\). Some introductory treatments simplify further and give a whole layer one shared bias scalar. Nothing in the derivations changes except the shape of one object, so this guide uses the general per-neuron vector \(b^l\) throughout.

Activations, and the zeroth layer

The outputs of a layer's neurons stack into the activation vector \(a^l\), and that vector is the input to layer \(l+1\). The input data is then just the zeroth activation:

\[ a^0 = \mathbf x \]

which is a small piece of bookkeeping that pays off later: it lets one formula cover every layer, including the first, with no special case for the input.

Self-check

A network takes 5 inputs, then has layers of 4, 3 and 1 neurons. What shapes are \(W^1\) and \(W^2\)?

\(W^1\) is \(4\times5\) and \(W^2\) is \(3\times4\), since \(W^l\) is \(n_l\times n_{l-1}\).

Check \(W^2\) carefully: the superscript 2 means "arriving at layer 2", so its 3 rows count the destinations in layer 2 and its 4 columns count the sources in layer 1. The input count of 5 never appears in \(W^2\) — it belongs to \(W^1\) alone.

What does \(w^3_{21}\) connect?

Neuron 1 of layer 2 to neuron 2 of layer 3. Destination \(j=2\) is written first, source \(k=1\) second, and the superscript names the layer the edge lands in — so the edge leaves layer \(3-1=2\).

Why is the cost a scalar rather than a vector?

Because minimisation needs an ordering, and vectors have no natural one. Reducing performance to a single number is what lets us say one parameter setting is better than another, and it is also what makes \(\nabla_\theta C\) a vector rather than a matrix — a function \(\mathbb R^{|\theta|}\to\mathbb R\) has a gradient, not a Jacobian.

Training changes the parameters but never the inputs. Where does that show up in the mathematics?

In which variable we differentiate with respect to. We compute \(\partial C/\partial\theta\) and never \(\partial C/\partial \mathbf x\). The inputs are constants of the problem — which, as chapter 4 shows, is why they end up appearing as multiplicative factors inside the weight gradients.
