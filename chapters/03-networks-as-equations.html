Chapter 3

Neurons, layers, forward propagation

One weighted sum, then many at once, then the whole network — written as equations and worked through with actual numbers, ending at the cost function that training will try to reduce.

In Simple Terms · The Big Picture

From One Neuron to an Entire Network

  • The Neuron is an Adder and a Gate: It takes inputs, scales them by weights, adds a bias, and passes the total through an activation function. If the total is high enough, it fires!
  • Why Non-Linearity (\(\phi\)) is Essential: Without activation functions, 100 stacked layers would mathematically collapse into a single flat linear layer. Non-linearities (like ReLU) bend decision boundaries so networks can learn non-linear shapes.
  • Matrix Notation is Parallelism: Instead of writing 1,000 separate equations for 1,000 neurons, we write \(z = W a + b\). One single matrix multiplication evaluates an entire layer of neurons simultaneously.

A single neuron

Physical Intuition: Water Pipes, Pressure Reservoir & Spring Valve

To visualize why a neuron behaves the way it does, imagine a hydraulic plumbing system:

  • Incoming pipes feed water at pressure levels \(x_1, x_2, x_3\).
  • Each pipe has a manual control valve that constricts or widens the flow — these are the weights \(w_i\). Negative weights act like suction pumps.
  • All water empties into an airtight accumulation chamber: the total pressure inside is the sum \(\sum w_i x_i\).
  • A spring-loaded pressure relief valve holds the chamber shut with resistance force \(-b\) (the bias).
  • The ReLU Gate: If total water pressure is below the spring force (\(z \le 0\)), the valve stays shut. Zero water exits (\(a = 0\)). The instant water pressure overcomes the spring (\(z > 0\)), the valve pops wide open, and water flows out in direct linear proportion to the excess pressure!

Physical Intuition: Pressure summation & spring-loaded relief valve

Give a neuron five inputs \(x_1,\dots,x_5\), each arriving along an edge carrying a weight \(w_1,\dots,w_5\). The neuron multiplies each input by its own weight, adds the results, and adds a bias:

\[ z=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_iw_i+b=\mathbf w\T\mathbf x+b \]
Visual Formula Decoder · The Layer Affine Equation
\(\mathbf{z}^l\) = \(W^l\) · \(\mathbf{a}^{l-1}\) + \(\mathbf{b}^l\)
\(\mathbf{z}^l\) (Destination Pressures) Layer Pre-activations

Column vector of pre-activations for all \(n_l\) neurons in the current layer.

\(W^l\) (\(n_l \times n_{l-1}\)) Wire Connections

Grid of weights. Row \(j\) contains all incoming wires feeding neuron \(j\).

\(\mathbf{a}^{l-1}\) (Source Signals) Prior Activations

Outputs sent from previous layer neurons. Multiplies the weight matrix from the right.

\(\mathbf{b}^l\) (Threshold Shifts) Layer Biases

Individual baseline shifts for each destination neuron in layer \(l\).

Note what just happened to the dimensions. Two length-\(n\) vectors went in and a single number came out — the sum collapses everything. A neuron's linear part is a map \(\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R\), which is to say exactly the kind of function that has a gradient.

The bias \(b\) is a scalar attached to the neuron, added unconditionally. It shifts the threshold at which the neuron activates and is a learnable parameter like any weight.

Then \(z\) passes through a non-linear activation function:

\[ a=\phi(z) \]
Why the non-linearity is not optional

Without \(\phi\), a layer computes an affine map, and composing affine maps gives another affine map: \(W^2(W^1\mathbf x+b^1)+b^2 = (W^2W^1)\mathbf x + (W^2b^1+b^2)\). A hundred stacked linear layers would collapse into one, and no depth would buy anything. The activation function is what keeps the composition from flattening.

Choosing \(\phi\)

This guide runs its derivations with ReLU, following modern deep learning convention and the Parr–Howard matrix calculus framework, because its derivative is trivial and the piecewise structure is instructive. The common alternatives, with the derivatives you will need in chapter 6:

Name\(\phi(z)\)\(\phi'(z)\)Note
ReLU\(\max(0,z)\)\(0\) if \(z<0\), \(1\) if \(z>0\)Undefined at \(0\); implementations pick 0 and never notice.
Sigmoid\(\sigma(z)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\)\(\sigma(z)\bigl(1-\sigma(z)\bigr)\)Peaks at \(0.25\), so gradients shrink through depth.
tanh\(\tanh z\)\(1-\tanh^2 z\)Zero-centred; peaks at 1.

From one neuron to a layer

Put three neurons side by side and feed all five inputs to each of them. Every neuron has its own five weights and its own bias, and each produces one scalar. Three scalars stack into a vector — and that vector is the input to the next layer.

Writing three separate dot products is wasteful when a single matrix product does the same work. Collect the weights into a matrix \(W^l\) with, as established in chapter 1, rows indexed by destination neuron \(j\) and columns by source neuron \(k\). Then:

\[ z^l = W^l a^{l-1}+b^l, \qquad a^l=\phi(z^l) \]

Row \(j\) of \(W^l\) holds exactly the weights feeding neuron \(j\), so row \(j\) of the product is row \(j\) dotted with \(a^{l-1}\) — precisely the weighted sum that neuron \(j\) wanted. The matrix form is not a different computation. It is the same \(n_l\) dot products, issued at once.

Figure 3.3: Layer forward pass in matrix form. Each row of \(W^l\) is the weight vector of one neuron, dotted with the incoming activations \(a^{l-1}\), plus that neuron's bias.
Shape check

\(\underbrace{W^l}_{n_l\times n_{l-1}}\;\underbrace{a^{l-1}}_{n_{l-1}\times1}\;+\;\underbrace{b^l}_{n_l\times1}\;=\;\underbrace{z^l}_{n_l\times1}\)

The inner \(n_{l-1}\) cancels. The surviving \(n_l\) is the width of this layer, which is also what \(b^l\) and \(z^l\) must be. If your bias has the wrong length, this line finds it.

Worked by hand

A two-input, two-neuron layer. Suppose the four weights are: \(x_1\to\) neuron 1 is 5, \(x_2\to\) neuron 1 is 2, \(x_1\to\) neuron 2 is 3, \(x_2\to\) neuron 2 is 6. Place each into row \(j\), column \(k\):

\[ W^1=\begin{bmatrix}5&2\\ 3&6\end{bmatrix} \]

Entry \((1,1)=5\) comes from source 1 and lands at destination 1. Entry \((1,2)=2\) comes from source 2 and still lands at destination 1 — same row, because the row is the destination. Now push the input \(a^0=\begin{bmatrix}10\\20\end{bmatrix}\) through, with a shared bias of 6:

  1. Matrix product.
    \[ W^1a^0=\begin{bmatrix}5&2\\ 3&6\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}10\\ 20\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5(10)+2(20)\\ 3(10)+6(20)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}90\\ 150\end{bmatrix} \]
    Row 1 is the dot product of neuron 1's weights with the inputs — the same arithmetic a single neuron does.
  2. Add the bias.
    \[ z^1=\begin{bmatrix}90\\ 150\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}6\\ 6\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}96\\ 156\end{bmatrix} \]
  3. Activate.
    \[ a^1=\operatorname{ReLU}(z^1)=\begin{bmatrix}96\\ 156\end{bmatrix} \]
    Both entries are positive, so ReLU passes them unchanged. Sigmoid would have flattened both to within a hair of 1 — a good illustration of why ReLU became the default.

\(a^1\) is now the input to layer 2, and the same three steps repeat with \(W^2\) and \(b^2\).

Forward propagation

Chaining the layer equation gives the forward pass. Written as a chain of substitutions, which is far more useful for deriving things than a picture of circles:

\[ a^1=\phi\bigl(W^1a^0+b^1\bigr) \]
\[ a^2=\phi\bigl(W^2a^1+b^2\bigr)=\phi\Bigl(W^2\phi\bigl(W^1a^0+b^1\bigr)+b^2\Bigr) \]

and so on to \(a^L=\hat y\). Equation 3.6 makes the nesting visible: the network really is one composed function, with the parameters buried at different depths. Getting a derivative out with respect to \(W^1\) means reaching through every layer above it.

Forward propagation
  1. Set \(a^0=\mathbf x\).
  2. For \(l=1,\dots,L\): compute \(z^l=W^la^{l-1}+b^l\), then \(a^l=\phi(z^l)\).
  3. Read off the prediction \(\hat y=a^L\).

A trained network needs nothing more than this: feed an image in as \(x_1,\dots,x_n\), read a classification out. Everything in the remaining chapters exists to produce good values of \(W^l\) and \(b^l\), after which forward propagation is the whole of what the model does.

The cost function

Before training, the weights are random and \(\hat y\) is worthless. To improve it we need a number that says how worthless. That is the cost function, and this guide uses mean squared error:

\[ C=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(y^{(i)}-a^{L,(i)}\bigr)^{2} \]

Read it left to right: for each of the \(m\) training examples, take the gap between the true answer \(y^{(i)}\) and the network's prediction \(a^{L,(i)}\), square it, sum over all examples, then average.

Why the square

It makes every error positive, so overshooting by 3 and undershooting by 3 cannot cancel out. It also penalises large errors disproportionately, and it is smoothly differentiable everywhere — unlike the absolute value, which has a corner at zero.

Why the \(\tfrac12\)

Pure convenience, and it costs nothing. Differentiating a square brings down a factor of 2 by the power rule, and that 2 cancels the \(\tfrac12\):

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial a}\;\frac12(y-a)^2=\tfrac12\cdot 2(y-a)\cdot(-1)=-(y-a)=a-y \]

Scaling a cost by a positive constant moves the minimum nowhere, so we are free to pick the constant that makes the derivative tidiest. The result — the derivative of the cost with respect to the prediction is simply prediction minus target — is the cleanest possible starting point for backpropagation.

Why the mean

Without \(1/m\), the cost of a million-example dataset would be a million times larger than the cost of the same model on one example, and a learning rate tuned on one dataset would be nonsense on another. Averaging makes the objective comparable across dataset sizes.

Sign convention

\((y-a)^2\) and \((a-y)^2\) are the same number, so the cost does not care which way round you write it. The derivative very much does — the two differ by a sign.

Chapter 4 derives with \(y-a\), carrying the minus sign through explicitly; chapter 6 uses \(a-y\), which absorbs it. Both reach the same place. When you write this yourself, pick one orientation and never switch mid-derivation, because a flipped sign in the gradient turns descent into ascent and the loss climbs instead of falling.

Many examples at once

The cost sums over \(m\) examples, so we need notation for a whole training set. Stack the examples as columns:

\[ X=\begin{bmatrix}x^{(1)}_1&x^{(2)}_1&\cdots&x^{(m)}_1\\ x^{(1)}_2&x^{(2)}_2&\cdots&x^{(m)}_2\\ \vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\ x^{(1)}_n&x^{(2)}_n&\cdots&x^{(m)}_n\end{bmatrix} \]

Superscripts in parentheses index the example, subscripts index the variable. So \(X\) is \(n\times m\): rows are features, columns are examples. Each column has its own known answer \(y^{(i)}\), which is what makes this supervised learning.

The pleasant consequence is that \(W^1X\) forward-propagates the entire training set in one matrix product, column by column, with no loop. That is the shape convention's real payoff and it is why this ordering is worth the initial awkwardness.

Where we are

We can now push an input through a network and score the result. Everything is in place to ask the only question that matters: how does \(C\) change when a single weight changes? Chapter 4 answers it completely for one neuron.

Self-check

A layer has \(W^l\) of shape \(7\times4\). How many neurons are in layer \(l\), and in layer \(l-1\)?

Seven in layer \(l\), four in layer \(l-1\). Rows are destinations, columns are sources. The layer also has 7 biases, giving \(28+7=35\) parameters.

Why can the bias not be folded into the weights?

It can, and often is. Append a constant 1 to the input vector and give it a weight; that weight then behaves exactly as the bias. The trick keeps the algebra to a single matrix product and shows up in older texts.

This guide keeps \(b\) separate because the bias derivative comes out different in kind from the weight derivative — chapter 4 shows \(\partial C/\partial b\) is the bare error while \(\partial C/\partial \mathbf w\) is the error scaled by the inputs — and that contrast is worth seeing rather than hiding.

What would happen if every activation function were the identity?

The network would collapse to a single affine map. \(W^2(W^1x+b^1)+b^2=(W^2W^1)x+(W^2b^1+b^2)\), and by induction any depth reduces to one effective weight matrix and one effective bias. Depth would be pure waste and the model could only ever fit a linear relationship.

In \(C=\frac{1}{2m}\sum(y-a^L)^2\), what is \(\partial C/\partial a^L\) for a single example?

\(a^L-y\). The \(\tfrac12\) cancels the 2 from the power rule and the minus sign from the inner derivative of \(y-a^L\) flips the order. This is the first quantity backpropagation computes, and chapter 6 calls it \(\nabla_{a}C\).
