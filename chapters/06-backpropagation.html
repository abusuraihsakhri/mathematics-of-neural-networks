Chapter 6

Backpropagation

Chapter 4 differentiated one neuron. Here the same calculus is organised so it works at any depth, in a single backward sweep: the error of a neuron, four equations, and the algorithm they assemble into.

In Simple Terms · The Big Picture

Backpropagation is corporate blame allocation:

  • The Core Problem: If the company loses money (Cost \(C\)), who is responsible? The executive at the output doesn't know what every frontline worker did. Backpropagation computes the exact blame (\(\delta\)) for every single neuron, working backwards from top managers down to the factory floor!
  • The Four Fundamental Equations (BP1–BP4):
    1. BP1 (Output Blame): \(\delta^L = \nabla_a C \odot \phi'(z^L)\) — Direct prediction error at the final output.
    2. BP2 (Blame Pullback): \(\delta^l = ((W^{l+1})\T \delta^{l+1}) \odot \phi'(z^l)\) — Pulls blame backward across layers through transposed weight matrices.
    3. BP3 (Bias Blame): \(\nabla_b C = \delta\) — Neuron biases absorb error directly.
    4. BP4 (Weight Blame): \(\nabla_W C = \delta (a^{\text{in}})\T\) — Blame on a connection equals the outgoing error times the incoming activity!

Corporate Blame Delegation

  • CEO at Output: Compares company revenue against budget target ($a^L - y$).
  • Vice Presidents: Scolded in proportion to how much their division contributed ($(W^{L})\T \delta^L$).
  • Frontline Workers: Held accountable only if their advice was followed and the outcome was disastrous!

Mathematical Backpropagation

  • BP1: $\delta^L = (a^L - y) \odot \phi'(z^L)$ seeds the error at the loss boundary.
  • BP2: $\delta^l = ((W^{l+1})\T \delta^{l+1}) \odot \phi'(z^l)$ pulls error backward layer-by-layer.
  • BP4: $\nabla_{W^l} C = \delta^l (a^{l-1})\T$ scales weight updates by input activations.

Why not just go forwards?

Figure 6.1: Spatial architecture of the four backpropagation equations. BP1 computes error at the output layer; BP2 pulls that error backward layer-by-layer; BP3 and BP4 convert errors into bias and weight gradients.
Physical Metaphor: The Corporate Blame Chain & Acoustic Megaphone

Consider a multi-tiered corporation: an executive compares the company's shipped product (\(\hat{y}\)) against market demand (Target \(y\)). The shortfall is \(\hat{y} - y\).

The executive doesn't bypass hierarchy to yell at every frontline factory worker individually. Instead, they assign blame to division managers in proportion to how heavily each division contributed (via transposed connection weights \((W)\T\)). Each division manager then reprimands their team leads, who reprimand frontline workers.

BP4 specifies the exact parameter update: if an employee shouted a loud recommendation (\(a_k\) was huge) and the receiving manager incurred massive error (\(\delta_j\) was large), that specific link is severely held accountable! If the employee was silent (\(a_k = 0\)), their connection receives zero blame.

Take a network with three layers — three inputs, then three neurons, then four, then one output. To find how the cost depends on the first weight matrix, the chain rule offers an obvious route:

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial W^1}=\frac{\partial C}{\partial a^3}\;\frac{\partial a^3}{\partial a^2}\;\frac{\partial a^2}{\partial a^1}\;\frac{\partial a^1}{\partial W^1} \]

Correct, and it works. Now do \(W^2\):

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial W^2}=\frac{\partial C}{\partial a^3}\;\frac{\partial a^3}{\partial a^2}\;\frac{\partial a^2}{\partial W^2} \]

Notice the waste. The factors \(\partial C/\partial a^3\) and \(\partial a^3/\partial a^2\) were already computed for 6.1 and are being recomputed here. Do this for every parameter in a deep network and most of the arithmetic is repetition — the same upper-layer sensitivities calculated again and again, once per parameter below them.

The cost argument, stated properly

This is not just inelegance. Sweeping forwards, each parameter needs its own pass through the layers above it, so computing all \(P\) partial derivatives costs roughly \(P\) passes. For a network with a million parameters, that is a million passes per training step.

Sweeping backwards, one forward pass plus one backward pass yields all \(P\) derivatives, and the backward pass costs about the same as the forward one. So the whole gradient is obtained for roughly twice the price of a single prediction — independent of \(P\).

That ratio is the entire reason deep learning is computationally possible. Backpropagation is the neural-network name for reverse-mode automatic differentiation, and this is its defining property: cheap when outputs are few and parameters are many, which is exactly the shape of a machine learning problem.

Going backwards, we accumulate. Start at the cost, walk one layer back and record what we learn, walk another layer back reusing what we recorded. Each layer is visited once. What gets accumulated and carried is the quantity defined next.

The error of a neuron

Definition

The error of neuron \(j\) in layer \(l\) is the sensitivity of the cost to that neuron's pre-activation:

\[ \delta^l_j \;=\; \frac{\partial C}{\partial z^l_j} \]

Stacked over the layer, \(\delta^l\) is an \(n_l\times1\) vector.

Two things about this definition deserve a pause.

Why \(z\) and not \(a\)? Mostly convenience — the algebra comes out cleaner — and it also places the quantity at exactly the right point in the chain. Every parameter of neuron \(j\) feeds into \(z^l_j\) and nothing else, so once you know how \(C\) responds to \(z^l_j\), one short step gets you to the weights and the bias. Defining the error at \(a\) would leave the activation derivative dangling on the wrong side of every subsequent formula.

Why call it "error"? Read the derivative as a claim about blame. If \(\delta^l_j\approx0\), perturbing this neuron's pre-activation barely moves the cost: the neuron is not responsible for much of the current error. If \(\delta^l_j\) is large, a small change here swings the cost substantially — this neuron amplifies whatever passes through it, and a large share of the total error can be attributed to it.

A more accurate name

"Error of a neuron" is conventional but slightly misleading; "influence of a neuron on the cost" is what the derivative actually measures. Picture the total cost as a pie divided among the neurons: \(\delta^l_j\) is the size of neuron \(j\)'s slice. Backpropagation is the procedure that cuts the pie, layer by layer, from the output end backwards.

BP1 — the error in the output layer

Start where the answer is known. At the final layer, \(a^L\) is compared directly against \(y\), so \(\partial C/\partial a^L\) is available immediately from the cost function. The definition of \(\delta\) wants \(z^L\) though, so one chain-rule link bridges the gap:

\[ \delta^L_j=\frac{\partial C}{\partial z^L_j}=\frac{\partial C}{\partial a^L_j}\;\frac{\partial a^L_j}{\partial z^L_j}=\frac{\partial C}{\partial a^L_j}\,\phi'(z^L_j) \]

Collecting all \(j\) into vectors, and recalling from chapter 2 that a diagonal Jacobian applied to a vector is an element-wise product:

\[ \delta^L=\nabla_{a}C\;\odot\;\phi'(z^L) \]

Both factors are known the instant the forward pass finishes. \(\phi'(z^L)\) comes from the stored pre-activations, and \(\nabla_a C\) comes from the cost function — for mean squared error, chapter 3 computed it:

\[ C=\tfrac12\lVert a^L-y\rVert^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nabla_a C=a^L-y \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \delta^L=(a^L-y)\odot\phi'(z^L) \]
Pairing the loss with the activation

The \(\phi'(z^L)\) factor can hurt. With a sigmoid output that is confidently wrong, \(z^L\) is far from zero, \(\sigma'(z^L)\approx0\), and \(\delta^L\) is crushed toward zero — the network learns slowest exactly when it is most wrong.

Pairing softmax output with cross-entropy loss removes the problem outright: the two derivatives cancel and BP1 collapses to \(\delta^L = a^L-y\), with no \(\phi'\) factor at all. That cancellation, not tradition, is why classification networks pair those two.

BP2 — pushing the error back a layer

This is the equation that makes the algorithm an algorithm. Given the errors in layer \(l+1\), it produces the errors in layer \(l\):

\[ \delta^l=\Bigl(\bigl(W^{l+1}\bigr)\T\,\delta^{l+1}\Bigr)\;\odot\;\phi'(z^l) \]

Combine it with BP1 and every layer is reachable: BP1 gives \(\delta^L\), BP2 gives \(\delta^{L-1}\), then \(\delta^{L-2}\), all the way down.

First reading: forward propagation, reversed

Recall that \(W^{l+1}\) has rows indexed by destination and columns by source. Transposing swaps them, so \((W^{l+1})\T\) has rows indexed by the neurons the signal came from. Multiplying by it moves a quantity from layer \(l+1\) back to layer \(l\) — the same connections traversed in the opposite direction.

Forward propagation pushes activations through \(W\); backpropagation pulls errors through \(W\T\). Then \(\odot\,\phi'(z^l)\) accounts for the activation function sitting in the path. The symmetry is not a coincidence, and the next derivation shows why.

Second reading: fill in the chain

Take a two-layer network written as a chain of substitutions:

\[ z^1=W^1a^0+b^1,\quad a^1=\phi(z^1),\qquad z^2=W^2a^1+b^2,\quad a^2=\phi(z^2) \]

Suppose \(\delta^2=\partial C/\partial z^2\) is already known and we want \(\delta^1=\partial C/\partial z^1\). Treat it as a puzzle: we have \(C\) over \(z^2\) and want \(C\) over \(z^1\), so we need intermediate links whose numerators and denominators cancel down to what we want.

  1. Find the route. What connects \(z^2\) back to \(z^1\)? Only \(a^1\) — it is the output of layer 1 and the input of layer 2.
    \[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial z^1}=\underbrace{\frac{\partial C}{\partial z^2}}_{\delta^2}\;\frac{\partial z^2}{\partial a^1}\;\frac{\partial a^1}{\partial z^1} \]
    The intermediates cancel formally, leaving \(\partial C/\partial z^1\), which is \(\delta^1\).
  2. Differentiate through the affine map. In \(z^2=W^2a^1+b^2\), the bias is a constant and the rest is a matrix acting on \(a^1\), so the Jacobian is the matrix itself:
    \[ \frac{\partial z^2}{\partial a^1}=W^2 \]
    Entry \((j,k)\) asks how \(z^2_j\) responds to \(a^1_k\); since \(z^2_j=\sum_k w^2_{jk}a^1_k+b^2_j\), the answer is \(w^2_{jk}\).
  3. Differentiate through the activation. \(\phi\) is element-wise, so the Jacobian is diagonal:
    \[ \frac{\partial a^1}{\partial z^1}=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\phi'(z^1)\bigr) \]
  4. Assemble — and mind the transposes. Jacobians compose acting on the left of column vectors, so pulling a gradient backwards through a map applies the transpose of its Jacobian:
    \[ \delta^1=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\phi'(z^1)\bigr)\,\bigl(W^2\bigr)\T\,\delta^2 \]
    If \(z^{l+1}=f(a^l)\) then \(\nabla_{a^l}C=J_f\T\,\nabla_{z^{l+1}}C\). The transpose is what turns "how outputs depend on inputs" into "how input-blame follows from output-blame".
  5. Rewrite the diagonal as an element-wise product. For any vectors, \(\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf u)\mathbf v=\mathbf u\odot\mathbf v\):
    \[ \delta^1=\Bigl(\bigl(W^2\bigr)\T\delta^2\Bigr)\odot\phi'(z^1) \]
    Which is BP2 with \(l=1\), arrived at from the chain rule alone.
Where the transpose really comes from

It is common to see this step justified with "the derivative of \(W\mathbf a\) with respect to \(\mathbf a\) is \(W\T\)". That is not quite right and it is worth being precise, because the imprecision makes the equation look arbitrary.

The Jacobian of \(\mathbf a\mapsto W\mathbf a\) is \(W\), full stop. The transpose appears because we are propagating a gradient — a column vector of sensitivities of a scalar — and gradients pull back through \(J\T\) while Jacobians push forward through \(J\). The shapes confirm it and nothing else needs to be taken on faith.

Shape check on BP2

\(\underbrace{(W^{l+1})\T}_{n_l\times n_{l+1}}\;\underbrace{\delta^{l+1}}_{n_{l+1}\times1}\;=\;n_l\times1\), then \(\odot\;\underbrace{\phi'(z^l)}_{n_l\times1}\;=\;\underbrace{\delta^l}_{n_l\times1}\)

One error per neuron in layer \(l\), as required. Had we used \(W^{l+1}\) untransposed, the inner dimensions would not have matched at all.

Vanishing and exploding gradients, read off BP2

Applying BP2 repeatedly means the error reaching layer \(l\) has been multiplied by a factor of \(\phi'\) once per layer it travelled through. With sigmoid, \(\sigma'\) never exceeds \(0.25\), so crossing \(k\) layers shrinks the signal by at least \(4^{-k}\). Ten layers is a factor of a million; early layers receive essentially nothing and stop learning.

That is the vanishing gradient problem, and BP2 is where it lives. Large weights produce the mirror failure, exploding gradients. Much of modern architecture design is a response to this one equation: ReLU keeps \(\phi'=1\) on the active branch, residual connections add a path where the multiplier is exactly 1, and careful initialisation keeps \((W)\T\) from scaling the signal up or down.

Figure 6.2: The vanishing gradient waterfall. With sigmoids, each backward step multiplies error by \(\phi'(z) \le 0.25\), cutting the gradient in quarters repeatedly. ReLU has a derivative of exactly 1 on active neurons, preventing gradient decay.

BP3 and BP4 — from errors to parameter gradients

The \(\delta\)s are not themselves what gradient descent consumes. Two short steps convert them.

BP3: the bias gradient

The bias of neuron \(j\) in layer \(l\) enters only through \(z^l_j=\sum_k w^l_{jk}a^{l-1}_k+b^l_j\), with coefficient 1:

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial b^l_j}=\frac{\partial C}{\partial z^l_j}\;\frac{\partial z^l_j}{\partial b^l_j}=\delta^l_j\cdot 1 \]
\[ \nabla_{b^l}C=\delta^l \]

The bias gradients are the errors. Not proportional to them, not derived from them — identical. Once the backward sweep is done, every bias gradient in the network is already sitting in memory.

BP4: the weight gradient

Same one step, with a different derivative at the end. From the same expression for \(z^l_j\), the sensitivity to \(w^l_{jk}\) is its multiplier \(a^{l-1}_k\):

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial w^l_{jk}}=\frac{\partial C}{\partial z^l_j}\;\frac{\partial z^l_j}{\partial w^l_{jk}}=\delta^l_j\,a^{l-1}_k \]

Which is the same statement as chapter 4's: a weight's gradient is an error term multiplied by the input flowing along that weight.

Now assemble the whole matrix. The gradient with respect to \(W^l\) must have the same shape as \(W^l\), so \(n_l\times n_{l-1}\). Writing out a \(3\times3\) case using 6.7:

\[ \nabla_{W^l}C=\begin{bmatrix}\delta^l_1a^{l-1}_1&\delta^l_1a^{l-1}_2&\delta^l_1a^{l-1}_3\\ \delta^l_2a^{l-1}_1&\delta^l_2a^{l-1}_2&\delta^l_2a^{l-1}_3\\ \delta^l_3a^{l-1}_1&\delta^l_3a^{l-1}_2&\delta^l_3a^{l-1}_3\end{bmatrix} \]

The pattern is unmistakable: \(\delta\) is constant along each row, \(a^{l-1}\) is constant down each column. Rows correspond to destination neurons, and every weight in a row feeds the same neuron, hence shares its error. Columns correspond to source neurons, and every weight in a column carries the same activation.

A matrix whose \((j,k)\) entry is \(u_jv_k\) is an outer product:

\[ \nabla_{W^l}C=\delta^l\bigl(a^{l-1}\bigr)\T \]
Shape check on BP4

\(\underbrace{\delta^l}_{n_l\times1}\;\underbrace{(a^{l-1})\T}_{1\times n_{l-1}}\;=\;\underbrace{n_l\times n_{l-1}}_{\text{exactly }W^l}\)

The inner 1s make this an outer product rather than a dot product. Written the other way round, \(a^{l-1}(\delta^l)\T\) would give \(n_{l-1}\times n_l\) — the transpose of what is needed. The shape check settles the ordering without having to remember it.

The algorithm

Four equations, assembled into a procedure. Note that steps 1 and 2 are the forward pass, and only steps 3–6 are backpropagation proper.

Backpropagation, for one training example
  1. Forward pass. For \(l=1,\dots,L\) compute \(z^l=W^la^{l-1}+b^l\) and \(a^l=\phi(z^l)\), storing every \(z^l\) and \(a^l\). All four equations need them, and recomputing later would waste the work.
  2. Evaluate the cost \(C(a^L,y)\).
  3. Output error. Apply BP1: \(\delta^L=\nabla_aC\odot\phi'(z^L)\).
  4. Backward sweep. For \(l=L-1,\dots,1\) apply BP2: \(\delta^l=\bigl((W^{l+1})\T\delta^{l+1}\bigr)\odot\phi'(z^l)\).
  5. Bias gradients. Apply BP3: \(\nabla_{b^l}C=\delta^l\). Free — they are the \(\delta\)s from step 4.
  6. Weight gradients. Apply BP4: \(\nabla_{W^l}C=\delta^l(a^{l-1})\T\), using the activations stored in step 1.

Steps 3 and 4 are the whole backward pass, and neither touches a weight gradient. Backpropagation computes errors; the parameter gradients fall out afterwards, almost as an afterthought, from the stored activations. That division is the clean idea at the centre of the algorithm.

Then unroll all the gradients into one vector, apply chapter 5's update \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\nabla_\theta C\), and repeat: forward, backward, update, forward, backward, update, for as many iterations as it takes.

Reconciling with chapter 4

Chapter 4 derived the one-neuron gradients the long way, through Hadamard products and sums and a piecewise ReLU. On the live branch, with a single example:

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial b}=e, \qquad \frac{\partial C}{\partial \mathbf w}=e\,\mathbf x\T \]

Set that network in the general notation. It has one layer, so \(L=1\); its input is the zeroth activation, so \(\mathbf x=a^0\); it has one output neuron, so \(\delta^1\) is a single number. On the active ReLU branch \(\phi'(z)=1\), so BP1 gives \(\delta^1=(a^1-y)\cdot 1=e\). Substituting into BP3 and BP4:

\[ \nabla_{b^1}C=\delta^1=e, \qquad \nabla_{W^1}C=\delta^1(a^0)\T=e\,\mathbf x\T \]

The same two lines. Chapter 4 and chapter 6 are not two results but one, seen from two distances.

What backpropagation actually generalises

In a one-neuron network the error is obvious: the neuron's output is compared directly against the answer, so its influence on the cost is visible by inspection. In a deep network, a neuron four layers from the output influences the cost only through everything downstream of it, and that influence cannot be read off.

BP2 is precisely the machinery for computing it anyway — carrying blame backwards one layer at a time so that every neuron ends up with the number that chapter 4 could simply see. Once each neuron has its \(\delta\), the weight and bias gradients are the same formulas as in the simple case.

The equations this laboratory implements, line by line

Forward pass, storing everything:

\[ z^1=W^1x+b^1,\quad a^1=\sigma(z^1),\qquad z^2=W^2a^1+b^2,\quad \hat y=\sigma(z^2) \]

Cost, and its derivative with respect to the output activation:

\[ C=\tfrac12(\hat y-y)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nabla_aC=\hat y-y \]

BP1, using \(\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))=\hat y(1-\hat y)\):

\[ \delta^2=(\hat y-y)\,\hat y(1-\hat y) \]

BP2, pulling the error back into the hidden layer:

\[ \delta^1=\bigl((W^2)\T\delta^2\bigr)\odot a^1\odot(1-a^1) \]

BP3 and BP4 for both layers:

\[ \nabla_{W^2}C=\delta^2(a^1)\T,\quad \nabla_{b^2}C=\delta^2,\qquad \nabla_{W^1}C=\delta^1x\T,\quad \nabla_{b^1}C=\delta^1 \]

Gradients are averaged over all four XOR examples, then applied with \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\nabla_\theta C\). Sigmoid is used rather than ReLU because a 2-neuron hidden layer with ReLU dies too often for a demonstration.

Where to go from here

The mathematics is complete. Three directions extend it.

Build one. Implement a network on MNIST digit classification with NumPy and nothing else — no PyTorch, no TensorFlow, no autograd. Write BP1–BP4 by hand and check every gradient against finite differences (equation 4.12). It is a weekend of work and it converts this material from something you have read into something you know.

Extend the theory. Backpropagation in convolutional networks, where weights are shared and the gradient of a shared weight sums over every position it appears in. In recurrent networks, where unrolling through time produces very long chains and BP2's multiplicative factors become a serious problem. And reverse-mode automatic differentiation in general, which is what a modern framework's autograd actually implements.

Use a framework, knowing what it does. PyTorch and TensorFlow automate this bookkeeping — you write the forward pass and .backward() runs BP1–BP4 over a graph it recorded. The automation is real, but the equations underneath are these four, unchanged.

Self-check

Why is \(\delta\) defined on \(z\) rather than on \(a\)?

Because every parameter of a neuron — all its weights and its bias — influences the cost only through that neuron's \(z\). Anchoring the error there means BP3 and BP4 are each a single multiplication away. Defining it at \(a\) would leave a \(\phi'\) factor to be reinserted in every downstream formula.

In BP2, why the transpose on \(W^{l+1}\)?

Two answers, and they agree. Structurally, \(W^{l+1}\) has rows indexed by destination and columns by source; transposing reverses that, so multiplying by \(W\T\) moves a quantity from the later layer back to the earlier one.

Formally, the Jacobian \(\partial z^{l+1}/\partial a^l\) is \(W^{l+1}\), and a gradient pulls back through the transpose of a Jacobian: \(\nabla_{a^l}C=J\T\nabla_{z^{l+1}}C\). The shapes confirm it — only \(n_l\times n_{l+1}\) times \(n_{l+1}\times1\) is defined.

Why must the forward pass store every \(z^l\) and \(a^l\)?

Because the backward pass consumes them. BP1 and BP2 need \(\phi'(z^l)\), and BP4 needs \(a^{l-1}\). They are computed anyway during the forward pass, so keeping them costs memory but no extra arithmetic — and recomputing them would mean re-running the forward pass at every layer.

This is a real memory cost at scale, which is why gradient checkpointing exists: store only some activations and recompute the rest, trading time for memory.

Layer \(l\) has 6 neurons, layer \(l-1\) has 10. What shapes are \(\delta^l\) and \(\nabla_{W^l}C\)?

\(\delta^l\) is \(6\times1\) — one error per neuron in layer \(l\). \(\nabla_{W^l}C\) is \(6\times10\), matching \(W^l\), and comes from the outer product \(\delta^l(a^{l-1})\T\): \((6\times1)(1\times10)\).

A 20-layer sigmoid network trains only in its last few layers. Which equation explains it, and what fixes it?

BP2. Every application multiplies the error by a factor of \(\sigma'\), which never exceeds \(0.25\). After 20 layers the signal has been scaled by at most \(0.25^{20}\approx10^{-12}\), so the early layers receive gradients indistinguishable from zero and their parameters never move.

Fixes attack the multiplier directly: ReLU has \(\phi'=1\) wherever it is active; residual connections add a path whose local derivative is exactly 1; normalisation layers keep pre-activations in the range where \(\phi'\) is not tiny; and careful initialisation stops \((W)\T\) compounding the shrinkage.
