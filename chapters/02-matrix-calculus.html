The matrix calculus toolkit

Five results, and every derivative in the rest of this guide is one of them or a product of them. Gradients, Jacobians, the chain rule in matrix form, and the derivatives of the element-wise operations a neuron is assembled from.

In Simple Terms · The Big Picture

The Calculus Toolkit: Three Everyday Tools

  • The Gradient (\(\nabla f\)): A 3D compass needle. If you stand on a hilly landscape, the gradient points straight up the steepest slope. Its length tells you how steep the hill is. Moving in the opposite direction (\(-\nabla f\)) leads down to the valley floor.
  • The Jacobian (\(J\)): A local elastic stretcher. When a function takes in multiple numbers and outputs multiple numbers, it stretches and skews space. The Jacobian matrix measures exactly how a tiny circle of input gets deformed into an ellipse.
  • The Chain Rule: Mechanical gears in series. If gear A turns gear B at 3×, and gear B turns gear C at 2×, turning gear A spins gear C at \(3 \times 2 = 6\times\). Derivatives multiply link-by-link down the chain.

Gradients: vector in, scalar out

A gradient is how we package the partial derivatives of a function that eats a vector and returns a single number, \(f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R\).

Figure 2.1: The geometry of the gradient on a topographic surface. At any coordinate \((x,y)\), \(\nabla f\) points in the direction of steepest ascent across the contour lines, while \(-\nabla f\) points straight downhill.
Physical Intuition: The Hiker in Dense Fog

Imagine standing on a mountain in pitch-black fog with zero visibility. You cannot see where the summit is, nor can you see the valley floor.

However, through the soles of your boots, you can feel the local tilt of the soil right where you stand. The direction pointing directly uphill against the tilt is \(\nabla f\). The angle of the tilt is the gradient magnitude \(\|\nabla f\|\).

If your goal is to descend safely into the valley, you don't need a global map. You simply take a step in the direction opposite to what your boots feel: \(-\nabla f\). That is the entire philosophy of gradient descent.

Take a concrete one:

\[ f(x,y)=x^2+\cos y \]

Two input variables, one output number. Feeding in \(x=2,\;y=0\) gives \(4+1=5\): a vector in \(\mathbb R^2\) has become a scalar in \(\mathbb R^1\). The partial derivatives are ordinary single-variable derivatives taken one variable at a time, everything else held fixed:

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=2x, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=-\sin y \]

Stack them and you have the gradient:

\[ \nabla f(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}\partial f/\partial x\\[2pt]\partial f/\partial y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2x\\-\sin y\end{bmatrix} \]

The symbol \(\nabla\) is a nabla, not a delta. With a thousand input variables the gradient would have a thousand entries: one per input, always. Its length is set by the input, never by the output, because the output is a single number.

Definition

The gradient of \(f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R\) is the \(n\times1\) vector of its first partial derivatives. Read it as a sensitivity profile: entry \(i\) says how fast \(f\) moves when input \(i\) moves, at this particular point.

Jacobians: vector in, vector out

Now let the output be a vector too, \(f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m\). There are more partial derivatives to keep track of — \(m\) output components, each differentiated with respect to \(n\) inputs, so \(mn\) of them — and they go into a matrix.

\[ f(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}2x+y^3\\ e^{y}-13x\end{bmatrix} \]

Split it into scalar component functions, \(f_1=2x+y^3\) and \(f_2=e^y-13x\), and differentiate each with respect to each variable. Four derivatives: \(2\), \(3y^2\), \(-13\), \(e^y\). Arrange them:

\[ J_f=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x}&\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial y}\\[10pt]\dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial x}&\dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial y}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&3y^2\\-13&e^{y}\end{bmatrix} \]
Figure 2.2: The geometric meaning of the Jacobian. A non-linear transformation locally stretches, shears, and rotates an infinitesimal circle into an ellipse. The Jacobian matrix contains the transformed coordinate axes.
Physical Intuition: The Stretched Rubber Sheet

Imagine drawing a perfect circular grid on a sheet of flexible silicone rubber. Now grab the corners and pull: stretch the x-axis, twist the edges, and shear the corners.

  • The circle deforms into a tilted, stretched ellipse.
  • The Jacobian matrix is the exact measurement of that distortion: column 1 says where the horizontal axis landed, and column 2 says where the vertical axis landed.
  • The determinant \(\det(J)\) measures area scaling: if \(\det(J) = 3\), that tiny patch of rubber expanded by 3×. If \(\det(J) = 0\), the patch collapsed completely into a flat line (information was destroyed).

Shape check

Rows are output components. Columns are input variables. A Jacobian is \(m\times n\) — outputs tall, inputs wide. Add an output and it grows downward; add an input and it grows sideways.

Each row is the gradient of one component function, laid on its side. Row \(i\) is \((\nabla f_i)\T\). A gradient is therefore a Jacobian with exactly one row, transposed — the two objects are the same idea at different output dimensions, which is why one set of chain-rule rules covers both.

Look for the zeros

In \(J_f\) above, \(\partial f_2/\partial x=-13\) is non-zero because \(x\) appears in \(f_2\). Had it not appeared, that entry would be zero. Zeros in a Jacobian are structural information: they say that this input has no route to that output.

This matters more than it looks. When \(f\) is applied element-wise — output \(i\) depends on input \(i\) and nothing else — every off-diagonal entry vanishes and the Jacobian is diagonal. Activation functions are element-wise, so this is the common case in a neural network, and it is what later lets whole matrix products collapse into cheap element-wise multiplications.

The scalar chain rule, rewritten as an algorithm

Figure 2.3: The chain rule as a mechanical gear train. Each intermediate variable acts as a transfer gear. The overall rate of change from input to output is simply the product of each gear's individual gear ratio.
Physical Intuition: Interlocking Mechanical Gears

Think of a 10-speed bicycle drivetrain:

  • Turning your pedals by 1 radian turns the front chainring by 1 radian.
  • The chainring turns the rear wheel cassette at a ratio of 3:1 (\(\partial g/\partial x = 3\)).
  • The rear wheel circumference turns the pavement distance at a ratio of 2.1 meters per rotation (\(\partial f/\partial g = 2.1\)).
  • How much pavement do you cover per pedal stroke? Multiply the gear ratios: \(3 \times 2.1 = 6.3\) meters!

In calculus, the chain rule is doing exactly the same mechanical arithmetic: multiplying sensitivities link by link down the transmission line.

Physical Proof: Why intermediate variables cancel out in the Chain Rule

You already know how to differentiate \(\sin(x^2)\): derivative of the inside times derivative of the outside, \(2x\cos(x^2)\). That shortcut does not generalise well. A slower, mechanical version does.

  1. Name the inner function. Let \(g=x^2\), so the whole thing is \(f=\sin g\). Every nested piece gets its own intermediate variable.
  2. Differentiate each link separately.
    \[ \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}=2x, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial g}=\cos g \]
  3. Multiply along the chain.
    \[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial g}\,\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}=\cos g\cdot 2x \]
    The intermediate cancels formally, which is the mnemonic the notation is built for.
  4. Substitute the intermediates back.
    \[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=2x\cos(x^2) \]

Same answer, more steps. The point is that this version does not care how deeply nested the function is. Given \(h(g(f(c(a(x)))))\), name every inner function, differentiate each link, multiply the lot, substitute back. A deep neural network is exactly that kind of nest, which is why the mechanical form is the one worth internalising.

The Jacobian chain rule

Visual Formula Decoder · Matrix Composition
\(J_{f \circ g}(\mathbf{x})\) = \(J_f(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}))\) · \(J_g(\mathbf{x})\)
\(J_{f \circ g}\) (Total Distortion) Combined Jacobian

How the entire pipeline transforms an input perturbation \(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\) into final output change \(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\).

\(J_f(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}))\) (Outer Link) Evaluated at \(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})\)

The downstream function. Appears on the left because matrices multiply on the left of column vectors.

\(J_g(\mathbf{x})\) (Inner Link) Evaluated at \(\mathbf{x}\)

The upstream function that processes the raw input first. Acts directly on the input vector.

Now run the same procedure where the links are vector-valued. The multiplication becomes matrix multiplication.

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf x}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf g}\;\frac{\partial \mathbf g}{\partial \mathbf x} \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad J_{f\circ g}(\mathbf x)=J_f(\mathbf g(\mathbf x))\,J_g(\mathbf x) \]
Order matters

Matrix multiplication does not commute: \(AB \ne BA\). The outer function's Jacobian goes on the left, the inner one on the right, and the inner-most factor ends up furthest right. Getting this backwards produces a matrix of the wrong shape, which is at least a loud failure — but in square cases it produces plausible nonsense instead.

Worked example

\[ f(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}\sin(x^2+y)\\ \ln(y^3)\end{bmatrix} \]
  1. Pull out the inner vector function.
    \[ \mathbf g(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}x^2+y\\ y^3\end{bmatrix}, \qquad f(\mathbf g)=\begin{bmatrix}\sin g_1\\ \ln g_2\end{bmatrix} \]
    Substituting \(\mathbf g\) back into \(f\) must reproduce the original.
  2. Jacobian of the inner function, with respect to \(x\) and \(y\):
    \[ \frac{\partial \mathbf g}{\partial \mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}2x&1\\ 0&3y^2\end{bmatrix} \]
    The zero is real: \(g_2=y^3\) contains no \(x\).
  3. Jacobian of the outer function, now with respect to \(g_1\) and \(g_2\):
    \[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf g}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos g_1&0\\ 0&1/g_2\end{bmatrix} \]
    Diagonal, because the outer function is element-wise: \(g_1\) touches only the first output.
  4. Multiply, outer on the left.
    \[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos g_1&0\\ 0&1/g_2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2x&1\\ 0&3y^2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2x\cos g_1&\cos g_1\\ 0&3y^2/g_2\end{bmatrix} \]
  5. Substitute back and simplify.
    \[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}2x\cos(x^2+y)&\cos(x^2+y)\\[4pt] 0&3/y\end{bmatrix} \]
    \(3y^2/y^3 = 3/y\).

Sanity-check the zero before moving on. It sits in row 2, column 1 — the sensitivity of the second output to \(x\). The second output is \(\ln(y^3)\), which has no \(x\) in it. Zero is right.

When a component has no inner function

Not every component of a vector function needs the chain rule. If \(f\) were \([\sin(x^2+y),\;\ln(y^3),\;xy^3]\), the third component has no inner function to peel off.

Give it the identity as its intermediate: set \(g_3 = xy^3\) so that \(\partial f_3/\partial g_3=1\). The 1 sits harmlessly in the outer Jacobian and the machinery runs unchanged. This comes up constantly, since real compositions are rarely uniformly nested.

Element-wise binary operations

A neuron is built from three operations, and two of them are element-wise. Their derivatives are worth having ready-made.

Definition

A binary element-wise operation takes two vectors of the same length and produces a third, where output component \(i\) depends only on input components \(i\):

\[ F(\mathbf v,\mathbf w)=\begin{bmatrix}f_1(\mathbf v)\;\bigcirc\;g_1(\mathbf w)\\ \vdots\\ f_n(\mathbf v)\;\bigcirc\;g_n(\mathbf w)\end{bmatrix} \]

The operator \(\bigcirc\) is what must be element-wise; the inner \(f_i,g_i\) are there for generality and are usually the identity.

The Hadamard product \(\mathbf v\odot\mathbf w\) is the case where \(\bigcirc\) is multiplication and the inner functions do nothing: component-wise multiply, \((v\odot w)_i=v_iw_i\). It shows up everywhere in backpropagation.

Because there are two input vectors, there are two Jacobians. Both are diagonal, for the reason established above — component \(i\) of the output cannot see component \(j\ne i\) of anything:

\[ \frac{\partial(\mathbf v\odot\mathbf w)}{\partial \mathbf v}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf w), \qquad \frac{\partial(\mathbf v\odot\mathbf w)}{\partial \mathbf w}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf v) \]

The scalar intuition survives intact: differentiating \(vw\) with respect to \(v\) gives \(w\). The only new content is that the result is spread down a diagonal, with structural zeros everywhere else. And because all the information lives on the diagonal, an \(n\times n\) diagonal matrix can be stored and applied as a length-\(n\) vector — an element-wise multiply instead of a matrix product.

Scalar expansion (broadcasting)

What is \(2\mathbf v\)? Formally a scalar has no business multiplying a vector element by element, so what actually happens — in the mathematics and in every array library — is that the scalar is expanded into a vector of the right length first, then an element-wise operation runs:

\[ \mathbf v \odot (\mathbf 1 z) \qquad\text{where}\qquad \mathbf 1 z = \begin{bmatrix}z\\ \vdots\\ z\end{bmatrix} \]

Two derivatives come out of this, and the second one is the interesting one.

With respect to \(\mathbf v\), it is the Hadamard result with \(\mathbf w=\mathbf 1z\):

\[ \frac{\partial(\mathbf v z)}{\partial \mathbf v}=\operatorname{diag}(z,\dots,z)=zI \]

With respect to \(z\) it is different in kind. \(z\) is a single number with no index, so there is nothing to vary sideways — the derivative can only run down the output components. The map is \(\mathbb R\to\mathbb R^n\), so its Jacobian is a single column:

\[ \frac{\partial(\mathbf v z)}{\partial z}=\mathbf v, \qquad \frac{\partial(\mathbf v + z)}{\partial z}=\mathbf 1 \]

Multiplication leaves \(\mathbf v\) behind; addition leaves ones; subtraction would leave \(-\mathbf 1\). All exactly the scalar rules, applied once per component.

The derivative of a sum

The last piece. Summing a vector's components is a map \(\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R\), so its derivative is a gradient:

\[ s(\mathbf v)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_i \]

Work out one entry. Differentiating with respect to \(v_1\), every term in the sum is a constant except \(v_1\) itself, which contributes 1. So the whole sum differentiates to \(1+0+0+\cdots=1\). Nothing distinguishes \(v_1\), so every entry is 1:

\[ \frac{\partial s}{\partial \mathbf v}=\mathbf 1\T=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\end{bmatrix} \]

If each component is scaled before summing, \(s(\mathbf v)=\sum_i zv_i\), the same argument gives \(z\mathbf 1\T\). The scaling factor simply rides along.

The identity that does the work in chapter 4
\[ \mathbf 1\T\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x\T \]

A row of ones times a diagonal matrix reads off the diagonal as a row vector. Since a dot product is a Hadamard product followed by a sum, this single line is what turns \(\partial(\mathbf w\T\mathbf x)/\partial\mathbf w\) into \(\mathbf x\T\) — the result that makes the input vector appear inside every weight gradient in the whole guide.

Self-check

\(f:\mathbb R^4\to\mathbb R^7\). What shape is its Jacobian, and what shape is the Jacobian of \(f\circ g\) where \(g:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^4\)?

\(J_f\) is \(7\times4\) — outputs tall, inputs wide. \(J_g\) is \(4\times2\). The product \(J_fJ_g\) is \(7\times4\) times \(4\times2\), so \(7\times2\): the inner 4s cancel, exactly as the composition \(\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^7\) demands. The shapes only line up in this order, which is a useful way to remember that outer goes left.

Why is the Jacobian of an activation function diagonal?

Because activation functions are applied element-wise: \(a_i=\phi(z_i)\) depends on \(z_i\) and on nothing else. So \(\partial a_i/\partial z_j=0\) whenever \(i\ne j\), leaving only \(\phi'(z_i)\) down the diagonal.

The practical consequence is large. Multiplying by \(\operatorname{diag}(\phi'(z))\) is the same as taking a Hadamard product with the vector \(\phi'(z)\) — \(O(n)\) work instead of \(O(n^2)\), and no \(n\times n\) matrix ever stored. This is why the backpropagation equations in chapter 6 are written with \(\odot\).

Compute \(\mathbf 1\T\operatorname{diag}(3,-1,4)\).

\(\begin{bmatrix}3&-1&4\end{bmatrix}\). Row \(i\) of the diagonal matrix contains only the entry \(d_i\), so summing down each column recovers the diagonal as a row vector. In general \(\mathbf 1\T\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x\T\).

A component of a vector function has no inner function. What do you put in its slot in the outer Jacobian?

A 1. Treat the component as its own intermediate, so the outer derivative with respect to that intermediate is 1, and the inner Jacobian carries the real derivative. The slot has to be filled with something — leaving it blank breaks the matrix, and a 0 would wrongly erase the component's contribution.
