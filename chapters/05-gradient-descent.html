Chapter 5

Gradient descent

We have derivatives. This chapter turns them into an algorithm: why moving against the gradient reduces the cost, what the learning rate actually controls, and why nobody computes the exact gradient on a real dataset.

In Simple Terms · The Big Picture

Gradient descent is a blind hiker feeling the slope of a mountain with their boots:

  • The Gradient (\(\nabla C\)): An arrow pointing straight uphill toward steepest ascent. To reduce cost, you simply step in the exact opposite direction (\(-\nabla C\)).
  • The Learning Rate (\(\eta\)): Your stride length. Take steps that are too tiny, and you will never reach base camp before nightfall. Take strides that are too gigantic, and you will leap across the valley and crash into the opposite cliff.
  • Mini-batching: Computing the gradient over all 1,000,000 examples takes minutes just for a single step (Batch GD). Computing it on 1 example is a wild, drunken stagger (Stochastic GD). Taking batches of 32 to 512 examples (Mini-batch) is the sweet spot: fast, GPU-friendly, and slightly noisy enough to bounce out of shallow potholes!

Physical World: Marble in Viscous Honey

  • Gravity pull: Pulls marble down slope (\(-\nabla C\)).
  • Viscosity friction: Resists motion, stopping runaway acceleration.
  • Marble Mass / Momentum (\(\beta\)): Built-up kinetic speed carries the marble smoothly along narrow ravines without jittering across walls!

Mathematical Optimization

  • Descent Direction: \(-\eta \nabla C\).
  • Damping coefficient: Prevents diverging overshoot.
  • Momentum Accumulator: \(\mathbf{v} \leftarrow \beta \mathbf{v} - \eta \nabla C\), then \(\theta \leftarrow \theta + \mathbf{v}\). Accumulates consistent directional gradient.

Physical Mechanics of the Loss Descent: The cost function creates a multi-dimensional topography of rolling hills and ravines. Physical Metaphor: The Rolling Marble in Viscous Honey & Momentum

Imagine the cost surface as an uneven bowl coated in thick honey. Placing a marble on the surface causes gravity to pull it directly downward along the path of steepest slope (\(-\nabla C\)).

Standard gradient descent is like a marble with zero inertia in infinite viscosity: the instant gravity stops or changes direction, the marble stops dead in its tracks. It can get hopelessly stranded on very flat plateaus or oscillate endlessly across narrow ravines.

Momentum (\(\beta\)) gives the marble mass and inertia! As it rolls down steep slopes, it accumulates velocity. When it enters a flat plateau or a narrow zigzagging ravine, its built-up forward momentum carries it smoothly along the valley floor toward the true minimum!

Take a neuron with two weights so the picture fits on a page. Plot \(w_1\) and \(w_2\) on the floor and the cost \(C\) vertically. Every point on the floor is a possible setting of the parameters, and above it sits the cost that setting produces — a curved surface, and training is the search for its lowest point.

At the current parameters the gradient is two numbers:

\[ \nabla_{\mathbf w}C=\begin{bmatrix}\partial C/\partial w_1\\ \partial C/\partial w_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}e\,x_1\\ e\,x_2\end{bmatrix} \]

Those two numbers can be read as the components of an arrow lying flat on the \((w_1,w_2)\) floor: go \(\partial C/\partial w_1\) in the \(w_1\) direction and \(\partial C/\partial w_2\) in the \(w_2\) direction. It is a direction in parameter space, not in the cost dimension — it says which way to walk, not how the ground rises.

The fact we lean on

The gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent of \(C\) at that point. So \(-\nabla C\) points in the direction of steepest descent — the direction that reduces the cost fastest per unit of movement.

Chapter 4's result makes this concrete: the arrow leans hardest toward whichever weight has the largest \(|e\,x_i|\). That is exactly the weight where a small change buys the biggest change in cost, which is what "steepest" means.

The update rule

Collect every weight and bias in the network into one long vector \(\theta\). The gradient \(\nabla_\theta C\) has the same length by construction — one partial derivative per parameter — so the two can be subtracted element by element:

\[ \theta \;\leftarrow\; \theta-\eta\,\nabla_\theta C \]

which, written out one parameter at a time, is just:

\[ w\leftarrow w-\eta\frac{\partial C}{\partial w}, \qquad b\leftarrow b-\eta\frac{\partial C}{\partial b} \]

The scalar \(\eta\) is the learning rate. Deceptively simple, and the entire optimiser.

Gradient descent is iterative. Parameters start random, so the initial cost is bad. Each step recomputes the gradient at the current position and nudges every parameter downhill. Repeat for thousands or tens of thousands of iterations and the cost falls — not always monotonically, but in trend — until it flattens out.

Unrolling

\(\theta\) is a bookkeeping convenience. The parameters really live in several matrices \(W^1,W^2,\dots\) and several vectors \(b^1,b^2,\dots\), of assorted shapes. To apply 5.2 we unroll them: flatten each matrix into a vector, concatenate everything into one long column, and do the same to the gradients so the layouts match.

After the update the vector is reshaped back into matrices for the next forward pass. Nothing mathematical happens here; it just lets one element-wise line describe the whole update.

The learning rate

The gradient supplies a direction. \(\eta\) decides how far to walk in it, and that choice matters more than its plainness suggests.

Figure 5.2: The four learning rate regimes. When \(\eta\) is too small, updates crawl imperceptibly. At the optimal rate, iterates converge rapidly. As \(\eta\) exceeds the stability threshold (\(\eta > 2/C''\)), iterates overshoot and diverge toward infinity.

The reason is a limitation baked into derivatives. A partial derivative describes the surface at a point — strictly, in an infinitesimal neighbourhood of it. It is a promise about the immediate vicinity and nothing more. Multiply that local information by a large \(\eta\) and you have extrapolated a local fact far beyond where it was valid. You can step clean over the minimum and land somewhere worse than you started.

  • \(\eta\) too large: steps overshoot, the cost oscillates or diverges outright.
  • \(\eta\) too small: every step is correct and negligible; training converges, eventually, at a rate nobody can afford.

There is no formula for the right value. \(\eta\) is a hyperparameter — set by the practitioner rather than learned by the algorithm — usually chosen by sweeping a few values across orders of magnitude and watching the loss curve.

One-dimensional gradient descent

\(C(w)=(w-2)^2+0.5\), minimum at \(w=2\)

Three values are worth trying. \(\eta=0.2\) converges smoothly. \(\eta=0.9\) overshoots the minimum every step but still converges, zig-zagging across it. \(\eta=1.1\) diverges: each step lands further from the minimum than the last, and the cost climbs forever. The boundary here is \(\eta=1\), because \(C''=2\) and descent on a quadratic is stable exactly while \(\eta<2/C''\).

Batch, mini-batch, stochastic

Equation 4.9 averages over all \(m\) training examples. That is batch gradient descent, and it is the mathematically honest version: every example votes on the direction before a single step is taken.

It is also, past a certain scale, unusable. With a million examples, one step means a forward pass, a backward pass and a gradient accumulation for each of the million — then one update. The second step repeats all of it. The arithmetic per unit of progress is brutal.

The fix is to accept an approximation. Shuffle the training set and cut it into small mini-batches — say 120 examples each. Compute the average gradient over one mini-batch, take a step, move to the next mini-batch, step again.

\[ \frac{1{,}000{,}000}{120}\approx 8333 \text{ mini-batches, hence } 8333 \text{ steps per pass over the data — against } 1 \]

The gradient from 120 examples is not the true gradient. The gambit is that 120 randomly chosen examples represent the full distribution well enough to give a direction that is roughly right, and that thousands of roughly-right steps beat one perfect one. In practice the gambit pays, decisively.

Shuffle first, and mean it

Mini-batching assumes each batch is a fair sample of the data. If the file happens to be sorted by label, the first batch is all one class, and the model lurches toward predicting that class before the next batch drags it elsewhere. The same applies to data sorted by date, by source, or by anything correlated with the target.

Shuffle before batching, and reshuffle between passes over the data.

A note on the names

The terminology is genuinely muddled and worth stating plainly.

MethodExamples per stepCharacter of the path

In ordinary usage "SGD" almost always means the middle row. The strict batch-size-one version is rarely what anyone runs, but it names the family, and the name stuck.

Picture the three on a contour plot of the cost. Batch descent walks a clean line to the minimum and takes forever to get going. Strict SGD staggers drunkenly but covers ground. Mini-batch wobbles mildly and arrives first — which is why it won.

Noise is not purely a cost

The noise in a mini-batch gradient has a genuine benefit: it can knock the parameters out of a shallow local minimum or off a saddle point that an exact gradient would have settled into. High-dimensional loss surfaces are full of saddle points, and a little stochasticity helps escape them.

This is also why modern optimisers do not simply lower \(\eta\) to zero. Momentum accumulates a running average of past gradients to push through flat regions; Adam additionally keeps a per-parameter running scale, giving each weight its own effective step size. Both are wrappers around equation 5.2 — the gradient they consume is still the one backpropagation computes.

Convergence

Repeat the loop — forward pass, backward pass, update — and watch the cost. A healthy run shows a steady decline, possibly with small upward blips from unlucky mini-batches, then a flattening. The flattening means the gradient has gone small: the parameters sit near a point where the surface is locally level.

That point is a local minimum, and it might not be the global one. In low dimensions this sounds alarming. In the millions of dimensions a real network occupies, it turns out to matter much less than intuition suggests, since most such networks find solutions of comparable quality from most starting points.

Where we are

We can optimise, given a gradient. For a one-neuron network chapter 4 supplies it. For a network of any depth we still have no efficient way to compute \(\nabla_\theta C\) — and that missing piece is backpropagation.

Self-check

Why subtract the gradient rather than add it?

The gradient points toward steepest increase of the cost. We want a decrease, so we move the opposite way. Adding it would be gradient ascent, a perfectly good algorithm for maximising something — just not what we want here.

\(\eta\) too large makes training fail. Why, in terms of what a derivative actually promises?

A partial derivative describes the surface in an infinitesimal neighbourhood of the current point. It is a local statement. Multiplying it by a large \(\eta\) extrapolates that local statement far outside the region where it holds, so the step can sail past the minimum and land somewhere higher. Repeat, and the iterates diverge.

A dataset has 50,000 examples and a batch size of 100. How many updates in one pass, and how many with batch gradient descent?

500 updates per pass with mini-batches; 1 with batch gradient descent. Both do roughly the same amount of gradient arithmetic per pass — the difference is that mini-batching spends it on 500 opportunities to improve instead of one.

Why must \(\theta\) and \(\nabla_\theta C\) have the same length?

Because 5.2 subtracts them element by element. Each parameter must line up with its own partial derivative. That is the entire reason for unrolling: the parameters live in matrices and vectors of different shapes, and flattening both sides identically is what makes a single subtraction well defined.
